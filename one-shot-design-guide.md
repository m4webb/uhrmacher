# One-Shot Adventure Design Guide

## Reference Principles for "The Uhrmacher's Folly"

*Compiled from research across experienced DMs, published adventure designers, and community best practices.*

---

## 1. Structural Framework

### The Three-Act Template

| Act | Time Budget | Purpose |
|-----|------------|---------|
| **Act I** | 15-20% (~45 min) | Hook, setting, stakes. Get the party moving fast. |
| **Act II** | 50-60% (~2.5 hrs) | Core encounters, rising tension, moral complexity. |
| **Act III** | 20-25% (~1 hr) | Climax and resolution. Protect this time fiercely. |
| **Buffer** | ~10% (~15-30 min) | Breaks, rules questions, table talk. |

### The One-Shot Formula
> **1 Meaningful Social Interaction + 2 Combat Encounters + 3 Distinct Locations = A Solid One-Shot**

Plan ~2 encounters per hour of play. "Encounter" means any scene requiring multiple dice rolls and table engagement (combat, social challenges, skill challenges, traps).

### Scene Flow: Alternating Beats
Design scenes with alternating tension -- rising and falling -- so players get emotional breathing room between intense moments:

1. Hook + immediate danger (UP)
2. Brief respite, NPC interaction, info gathering (DOWN)
3. Complication, harder challenge (UP)
4. Discovery, preparation moment (DOWN)
5. Climactic confrontation (BIG UP)
6. Resolution/denouement (DOWN)

## 2. Managing 8 Players

This is the single biggest operational challenge. Every design decision must account for it.

### Combat Speed
- **"On deck" system:** Announce current turn, next turn, and on-deck. Players get 2 turns to plan.
- **Roll attack and damage simultaneously.** Require this of all players.
- **Use average damage for monsters.** Pre-roll monster attacks during player turns.
- **One-sentence narration per turn.** Save the flowery descriptions for killing blows and crits.
- **Enforce turn time limits.** Not ready = Dodge action, move on. Establish this before play.
- **Count monster HP upward** (damage taken) instead of subtracting from total.
- **Target 3 rounds per combat.** A 5-round fight with 8 PCs can take 90+ minutes.

### Encounter Balancing for 8 PCs
- A single boss monster will be demolished in 2 rounds by the action economy. **Always include minions.**
- Use a roughly **2:1 monster-to-PC ratio** for big fights (~16 creatures).
- Consider running encounters **3 CR levels above party level** to compensate.
- Give boss monsters **legendary actions, lair actions, and environmental threats**.
- Distribute **defensive magic items** rather than offensive ones -- lets you use tougher monsters without one-shotting PCs.

### Table Management
- **Assign roles:** Mapper, loot tracker, note-taker, rules referencer.
- **Consider tactical party splitting** into two 4-person teams for simultaneous objectives. Bounce between groups on one initiative list.
- **Limit character introductions to 1 minute each.** 8 players x 5 minutes = 40 minutes lost.
- **Handle admin outside the session** (shopping, leveling, character questions via Discord/email beforehand).
- **Set expectations explicitly** at session start: fast-paced, quick turns, no side quests.

## 3. Encounter Design

### Combat Encounters
- Plan **2-3 combat encounters total.** More will eat all available time with 8 players.
- Target **"Hard" or "Deadly" difficulty.** With no long rest and one session, let players go nova.
- Include **interactive environmental elements in every fight:** collapsing floors, swinging pendulums, spreading effects, objects to use creatively.
- Include at least one encounter where the objective is NOT "kill everything" -- defend a point, escape, solve a puzzle during combat, protect an NPC.
- Use **difficult terrain, elevation changes, and tight spaces** to create tactical variety.

### Non-Combat Encounters
- At least **1 meaningful social interaction** resolved primarily through roleplaying, not dice.
- Design NPCs with **distinctive, broad mannerisms** (tics, accents, catchphrases). Subtlety is lost with 8 players -- go bold.
- Prepare ~3 NPCs with meaningful interaction opportunities, each relevant to the mission.

### Information Flow
- **Never gate critical info behind a single check or source.** Always have 2-3 ways for players to learn what they need.
- Complex investigation chains will consume the entire session. Give information quickly and get to the action.
- Every NPC, location, and detail you mention should connect to the plot. Extraneous details send players chasing tangents.

## 4. Moral Dilemmas

### Design Framework
A strong dilemma presents **two or more imperfect options**, each with real consequences. No clearly "right" answer.

**Key questions to answer during design:**
- If the PCs do nothing, what happens? (Inaction must have consequences)
- What do the PCs lose with Choice A? With Choice B?
- How does each choice benefit someone the PCs oppose?
- How does each choice hurt someone the PCs want to protect?

### Rules for One-Shot Dilemmas
- Consequences must be **visible and immediate.** No future sessions to show ripple effects -- make the fallout dramatic and fast.
- Avoid dilemmas that **split the party along character stereotypes** (rogues vs. paladins). Find dilemmas that unite the party in debate.
- Some townsfolk should advocate for EACH side. The players should hear compelling arguments for both options from NPCs they care about.
- Do not make one option clearly "good" and the other clearly "evil." Ethical ambiguity is what generates genuine discussion.

## 5. The Ticking Clock

A ticking clock is nearly essential for 8 players. Without it, the group will debate endlessly.

### Implementation Options
- **Explicit clock:** "The ritual completes at midnight. You have 6 hours." Track visibly with a prop or whiteboard.
- **Escalating consequences:** Every 30 real-world minutes (or every major scene), something gets worse.
- **Hard vs. soft deadline:** Hard = adventure fails if time runs out. Soft = things get harder but remain winnable.

### DM Pacing Tool
The clock is as much a pacing tool for the DM as a tension tool for players:
- If players are ahead of schedule, secretly add time or complications.
- If they are behind, skip a planned encounter and narrate it as a quick success.
- If a scene drags, the clock provides a natural reason to move things along.

## 6. Pre-Generated Characters

### Design Principles
- Keep characters at **level 3-5.** Complex enough to be interesting, simple enough to learn in 10 minutes.
- **Simplified one-page character sheets.** Only essential information. Skip passive perception, only list non-default skill bonuses.
- Let players choose spells from cards/books at the table rather than pre-selecting all of them.
- Allow cosmetic customization freely (name, gender, appearance, weapon names).

### Inter-Party Connections
- Pre-determine at least one **relationship** between each character and another PC. Write it on the sheet.
- Characters should already know each other or have a compelling reason to cooperate. Do NOT waste one-shot time on "why are we working together?"
- Replace Personality/Ideals/Bonds/Flaws with a **shared party setup** relevant to the adventure.

### Party Composition for 8
- Ensure coverage: at least 1 healer, 1 tank, 1 ranged damage, 1 social/face character.
- Give each character **one unique "moment to shine" ability** relevant to the adventure.
- Include 2-3 sentence personality prompts, not mandatory backstories.

## 7. Pacing Safeguards

### Plan for Flexibility
- **Prepare less than you think you need.** You can improvise more content but cannot compress a 6-hour adventure into 4 hours.
- **Identify optional encounters** that can be cut or added depending on pace.
- **Know what to cut.** Which encounters can be summarized? Which scenes can be narrated instead of played?

### Protect the Ending
- Reserve the final 45-60 minutes for Act III no matter what.
- If Act II runs long, cut an encounter and jump to the climax.
- A rushed but present ending is infinitely better than no ending at all.

### The "Hidden Rails" Technique
All paths should lead to the same major story beats -- the difference is HOW players get there, not WHERE they end up. Every path through the adventure should feel like a choice while still converging on the climax.

## 8. Common Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Solution |
|---------|----------|
| Too much content planned | Cut 30% of what you think you need |
| Complex investigation chains | Give info quickly, get to action |
| Single chokepoint for critical info | Always have 2-3 sources for key information |
| Not setting expectations | Brief players on pace and format before play |
| Overly long character intros | 1 minute per player, characterize through play |
| Combats running too long | Design for 3 rounds, use environment to force action |
| No contingency for slow pacing | Know what's optional, have a cut list |
| Campaign-sized story in one session | One villain, one location, one objective |
| Fizzled ending | Protect Act III time ruthlessly |
| Ignoring action economy (8 PCs) | Minions, legendary actions, lair actions, always |

## 9. Pre-Session Checklist

- [ ] Primary feeling/concept defined
- [ ] Hook and MacGuffin identified
- [ ] 3-act structure outlined with time estimates
- [ ] 2-3 combat encounters designed (Hard/Deadly, 3-round target)
- [ ] Boss fight has minions, legendary actions, and environmental threats
- [ ] 1+ meaningful social/roleplay scene
- [ ] 1+ moral dilemma with no clean answer
- [ ] Ticking clock mechanism chosen and integrated
- [ ] Environmental elements in every combat
- [ ] 3+ distinct locations with sensory descriptions
- [ ] Pre-generated characters with inter-party connections
- [ ] Optional encounters identified (cut list ready)
- [ ] Multiple paths to every critical piece of information
- [ ] Multiple endings fully developed
- [ ] Player expectations briefing prepared
- [ ] Combat speed rules decided and ready to communicate
